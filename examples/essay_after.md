Remote work changed two things at most companies that tried it: where people sit, and how much of the day goes to getting there. Everything else depends on how well a team writes things down.

In most surveys, employees who can work from home report higher satisfaction, mostly because they control their own schedule and skip the commute. For someone with an hour each way, that's ten hours a week back. Companies save money too, chiefly on office space, though few of them have actually given up their leases.

The costs are real but harder to count. Some people get lonely. New hires have it worst, because they can't overhear how the job is done. And when your kitchen is your office, it's hard to know when the workday ends. Teams that do well remotely tend to set explicit norms: when you're expected to answer, what goes in a document instead of a call, which meetings are worth having.

Most teams need only a shared doc and a chat app. The harder part is that remote teams can't rely on hallway conversation, so managers have to say out loud what used to be absorbed: who decides, what "done" means, and why a priority changed.

Whether it works depends on the job. If the work can be written down, remote is usually fine. If it relies on apprenticeship, or on people noticing problems by walking past them, it's a harder sell. [example needed: one concrete team from the author's experience]
