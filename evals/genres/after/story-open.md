By dawn the storm had worn itself out. The sea was a restless gray and the air tasted of salt. Elias Marlow climbed down from the lighthouse, his knees complaining on every step of the winding iron staircase, and pushed open the heavy door to see what was left.

Seaweed lay across the path. A shutter had been torn off the keeper's cottage and flung into the garden. That was nothing for a storm that size. Elias had weathered thirty-one winters on this stretch of rock, and he knew the sea's tempers as well as he knew his own.

Down near the water's edge, between two black boulders where the waves still licked and foamed, something caught his eye. He stopped. It was a boot.

He picked his way across the slick rocks and knelt to look at it. A man's boot of worn leather, the sole cracked and the laces still knotted tight, as though whoever wore it had never untied it at all. No fisherman in these parts would own one like it: the stitching was too fine, the leather too well made, and on the inside, in faded gold lettering, were the initials *T.H.*

Elias looked up and down the empty shoreline. He heard only the gulls and the slow pulse of the tide. Nobody had been out on those waters last night, and nobody could have been.

He turned the boot over in his hands, and something small slid out and clinked against the rock.
