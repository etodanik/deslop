Across America, cities say they want cheaper housing, but many keep a rule that makes it dearer: parking minimums. These rules force developers to build a set number of parking spaces for every new apartment, whether or not future residents need them. Each underground or structured space can add tens of thousands of dollars to a project, so housing costs more to build and therefore more to rent. Tenants pay that bill, including the many who don't own a car.

Parking also eats land that could hold homes, shops and public spaces. Where space is scarce and demand is high, giving so much of it to stored vehicles makes little sense. Fewer units fit on each lot, some projects don't pencil out at all, and neighborhoods get acres of asphalt where walkable streets could be. Minneapolis and Buffalo eliminated these requirements, and developers there have put up more housing and adapted it more readily to what residents want.

Critics worry that without minimums, streets will fill with cars and parking will run short. But repealing the mandate doesn't ban parking; it lets the market decide how much to build. Developers will still provide it where people want it, and cities can manage street parking with pricing and permits. If cities drop parking minimums, housing gets cheaper, more people can get by without a car, and land goes to people instead of cars. Cities should stop legislating how many cars to plan for and start planning for the people who need homes.
