Across America, cities are grappling with a housing affordability crisis, yet many continue to enforce a policy that quietly makes the problem worse: parking minimums. These rules require developers to build a set number of parking spaces for every new apartment, regardless of whether future residents actually need them. The result is housing that costs more to build, and therefore more to rent, as each underground or structured parking space can add tens of thousands of dollars to a project's price tag. Those costs are passed directly on to tenants, including the many who don't own a car.

Parking minimums also take up valuable land that could be used for homes, shops, and public spaces. In cities where space is limited and demand is high, dedicating so much real estate to storing vehicles is a remarkably inefficient use of resources. Fewer units can fit on each lot, projects become financially unviable, and neighborhoods end up with sprawling asphalt instead of vibrant, walkable streets. Cities that have eliminated these requirements, such as Minneapolis and Buffalo, have seen developers build more housing and respond more flexibly to what residents truly want.

Critics worry that removing minimums will lead to overcrowded streets and parking shortages. But repealing the mandate does not ban parking; it simply lets the market decide how much is appropriate. Developers will still build parking where there is demand for it, and cities can manage street parking through smart pricing and permits. By removing parking minimums, cities can lower housing costs, encourage sustainable transportation, and make room for the communities of the future. It is time to stop legislating how many cars we must plan for and start prioritizing the people who need homes.
