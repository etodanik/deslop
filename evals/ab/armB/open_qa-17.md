South Africa is a parliamentary republic, so voters don't elect their leaders directly the way Americans elect a president.

Citizens vote for political parties in national elections, held every five years. They choose from party lists, and since recent reforms independent candidates can also run. The 400 seats of the National Assembly are filled by proportional representation, so each party gets a share of the seats that matches its share of the vote. After the election, the Assembly elects the President from among its members, usually the leader of the majority party or coalition. That vote must happen within 30 days of the election. The President is both head of state and head of government, and can serve at most two five-year terms.

The other bodies work differently. The National Council of Provinces speaks for the provinces, and the provincial legislatures choose its delegates. Provincial legislatures are elected at the same time as the National Assembly and then choose each province's Premier. In local government, municipal councils combine ward representatives with party-list seats.

In 2024, no party won an outright majority for the first time since 1994. That produced a coalition "Government of National Unity" and the re-election of Cyril Ramaphosa as president.
