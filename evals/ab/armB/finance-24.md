Probably not directly, and even if it could, it's likely a bad idea for you and the company. What happens depends on the type of debt, your country, and the company's legal form.

The first problem is tax. If the company pays off your personal debt, the IRS (or your country's tax authority) will most likely treat the payment as wages to you, taxable and subject to payroll taxes. If you're an owner, it may count as a dividend or shareholder distribution instead. It could also be a loan to you, but then you'd need paperwork with interest and repayment terms. The company usually can't deduct personal expenses.

If you own a corporation or LLC, mixing personal and business money can weaken the limited liability protection (the "corporate veil"). A court can treat you and the company as one, and then creditors may go after business assets.

A company can technically buy a debt, the way debt buyers purchase it from a creditor at a discount. But if you control the company, regulators and tax authorities may see that as a sham. And if the debt is forgiven or bought at a discount, you may owe tax on "cancellation of debt" income.

If you're an employee and not an owner, the employer paying your debt is just extra pay. Some employers do offer student loan repayment assistance, up to $5,250 tax-free per year in the US under certain rules.

Three routes are cleaner. You can take a documented, interest-bearing loan from the company, though public companies in the US can't lend to executives under Sarbanes-Oxley, so check the limits. You can pay yourself a salary, bonus or distribution, pay the taxes, and use the money for the debt. Or you can use an employer program if you have one.
