Human intelligence is the mental capacity to learn from experience, reason about hard ideas, solve problems, adapt to new situations, think abstractly, and use knowledge to shape your environment. Psychology, neuroscience and philosophy all study it, and so, more and more, does artificial intelligence research.

No definition is universally accepted. Many researchers describe it as a general mental ability for reasoning, planning, problem solving, abstract thinking, grasping complex ideas, and learning quickly. Others stress adapting to one's environment or reaching goals in varied circumstances.

The main theories:

- **Spearman's g factor (general intelligence):** Charles Spearman observed that people who do well on one cognitive test tend to do well on others, and proposed a common factor, *g*, plus specific abilities.
- **Fluid and crystallized intelligence (Cattell-Horn):** Fluid intelligence is the ability to reason and solve novel problems; crystallized intelligence is accumulated knowledge and skills.
- **Cattell-Horn-Carroll (CHC) theory:** A hierarchical model combining many broad and narrow abilities.
- **Gardner's multiple intelligences:** Howard Gardner proposed several relatively independent intelligences (linguistic, logical-mathematical, spatial, musical, bodily-kinesthetic, interpersonal, intrapersonal, naturalistic). It's influential in education but has limited empirical support.
- **Sternberg's triarchic theory:** Intelligence has analytical, creative, and practical components.
- **Emotional intelligence:** The ability to perceive, understand, and manage emotions.

Standardized tests measure intelligence as an **intelligence quotient (IQ)**. Current IQ tests (such as the Wechsler scales and the Stanford-Binet) are normed so that the average score is 100 with a standard deviation of 15. IQ scores are fairly stable and predict outcomes like academic achievement and job performance, but they miss things such as creativity and practical skills.

Both genes and environment shape intelligence. Twin and adoption studies suggest heritability is high and rises with age, and nutrition, education, health and socioeconomic conditions matter too. The **Flynn effect** is the large rise in average IQ scores across the 20th century in many countries.

Research links intelligence to brain structure and function: brain size, how efficient neural networks are, and especially the frontal and parietal regions (the parieto-frontal integration theory).

Debates continue over what intelligence is, how much comes from genes versus environment, group differences in test scores, cultural bias in testing, and the ethics of testing.
