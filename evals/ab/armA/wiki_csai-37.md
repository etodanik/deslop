**Human intelligence** is the mental capacity that lets people learn from experience, understand and reason about complex ideas, solve problems, adapt to new situations, think abstractly, and use knowledge to manipulate their environment. Psychology, neuroscience and philosophy all study it, and so, increasingly, does artificial intelligence.

No single definition is universally accepted. Many researchers describe it as a general mental ability involved in reasoning, planning, problem solving, abstract thinking, comprehending complex ideas and learning quickly. Others stress adapting to one's environment or achieving goals in varied circumstances.

**Major theories**

- **Spearman's g factor (general intelligence):** Charles Spearman noticed that people who do well on one cognitive test tend to do well on others, and proposed a common underlying factor, *g*, plus specific abilities.
- **Fluid and crystallized intelligence (Cattell-Horn):** fluid intelligence is the ability to reason and solve novel problems; crystallized intelligence is accumulated knowledge and skills.
- **Cattell-Horn-Carroll (CHC) theory:** a hierarchical model that combines many broad and narrow abilities.
- **Gardner's multiple intelligences:** Howard Gardner proposed several relatively independent intelligences (linguistic, logical-mathematical, spatial, musical, bodily-kinesthetic, interpersonal, intrapersonal, naturalistic). Education has embraced it, but it has limited empirical support.
- **Sternberg's triarchic theory:** intelligence has analytical, creative and practical components.
- **Emotional intelligence:** the ability to perceive, understand and manage emotions.

**Measurement.** Standardized tests yield an **intelligence quotient (IQ)**. Modern tests such as the Wechsler scales and the Stanford-Binet are normed so the average score is 100 with a standard deviation of 15. IQ scores are fairly stable and predict outcomes like academic achievement and job performance, but they miss things such as creativity and practical skills.

**Genes and environment.** Both shape intelligence. Twin and adoption studies suggest heritability is substantial and rises with age, while nutrition, education, health and socioeconomic conditions also matter. The **Flynn effect** is the large rise in average IQ scores across the 20th century in many countries.

**Biology.** Research links intelligence to brain structure and function: brain size, how efficiently neural networks work, and especially the frontal and parietal regions (the parieto-frontal integration theory).

**Open debates.** Researchers still disagree about what intelligence is, how much genes and environment each contribute, why groups differ in test scores, how much cultural bias affects testing, and whether and how testing should ethically be used.
