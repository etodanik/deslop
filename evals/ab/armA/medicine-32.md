I'm sorry you're dealing with joint pain. I'm not a doctor and can't diagnose you, but I can explain what these results usually mean.

ASO stands for antistreptolysin O. The blood test looks for antibodies your body makes after a streptococcal (strep) infection, such as strep throat. A raised titre means you were probably exposed to group A streptococcus at some point recently, since the antibodies can stay elevated for weeks to months. On its own that doesn't confirm any disease, and plenty of healthy people, especially in some regions, have high titres. The result only means much alongside your symptoms and other tests.

It matters with joint pain because a strep infection can sometimes trigger rheumatic fever. That causes pain that moves from joint to joint, fever, a rash, and sometimes heart involvement, and doctors diagnose it with a set of criteria called the Jones criteria. The values you give (1:8, or about 1600 IU/mL) are on the high side, but your doctor has to weigh your whole picture.

Other things can cause joint pain at 29: reactive arthritis, rheumatoid arthritis or other autoimmune conditions, overuse injuries and mechanical problems, vitamin D deficiency, viral infections, and gout. Autoimmune and rheumatic conditions often run in families, so tell your doctor about your family history.

Tests that might help include ESR and CRP (inflammation markers), rheumatoid factor, anti-CCP, ANA, a throat swab, uric acid and vitamin D. If rheumatic fever is suspected, you'd also want an ECG and echocardiogram.

How severe and how curable depends on the diagnosis. For rheumatic fever, treatment is penicillin to clear any remaining strep bacteria, anti-inflammatory medicine for the joints, and sometimes long-term preventive penicillin to stop recurrences. The joint symptoms generally resolve completely, though any heart involvement needs follow-up. If it turns out to be something else, treatment varies and many of those conditions can be well controlled.

Next step: see your doctor or a rheumatologist and ask about further testing. Go urgently if you get chest pain, shortness of breath, swelling, fever or a rash.
