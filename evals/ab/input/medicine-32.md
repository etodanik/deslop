**What is an ASO test?**

ASO stands for **antistreptolysin O**. It's a blood test that checks for antibodies your body makes after a **streptococcal (strep) infection**, such as strep throat. A raised ASO titre means you were probably exposed to group A streptococcus at some point in the recent past (the antibodies can remain elevated for weeks to months).

**Does a positive ASO mean something serious?**

Not necessarily. A positive or high ASO test alone doesn't confirm disease. Many healthy people, especially in certain regions, have elevated titres. It's most useful when combined with symptoms and other tests.

**Why it matters with joint pain**

Sometimes, after a strep infection, the immune system can cause **rheumatic fever**, which can cause joint pain (usually moving from joint to joint), fever, a rash, and sometimes heart involvement. Doctors use a set of criteria (the Jones criteria) to diagnose it. The values you mention (1:8 or around 1600 IU/mL) are on the high side, but your doctor needs to consider your overall picture.

**Other possible causes of joint pain at 29**

- Reactive arthritis
- Rheumatoid arthritis or other autoimmune conditions
- Overuse injuries, mechanical problems
- Vitamin D deficiency
- Viral infections
- Gout, or other conditions

Hereditary tendencies are common in autoimmune and rheumatic conditions, so family history is worth telling your doctor.

**What tests might help**

- ESR and CRP (inflammation markers)
- Rheumatoid factor, anti-CCP, ANA
- Throat swab
- ECG and echocardiogram if rheumatic fever is suspected
- Uric acid, vitamin D

**Treatment and curability**

It depends on the diagnosis:
- If it's rheumatic fever, treatment includes **penicillin** to clear any remaining strep bacteria, anti-inflammatory medicine for the joints, and sometimes long-term preventive penicillin to avoid recurrences. Joint symptoms generally resolve completely, though heart involvement needs follow-up.
- If it's another condition, treatment varies, and many can be well controlled.

**What to do**

Follow up with your doctor or a **rheumatologist**, and ask about additional testing. See a doctor urgently if you have chest pain, shortness of breath, swelling, fever, or a rash.
