**Human intelligence** is the mental capacity that allows people to learn from experience, understand and reason about complex ideas, solve problems, adapt to new situations, think abstractly, and use knowledge to manipulate their environment. It is a central topic in psychology, neuroscience, philosophy, and, increasingly, artificial intelligence.

**Defining intelligence**

There is no single universally accepted definition. Many researchers describe it as a general mental ability involved in reasoning, planning, problem solving, abstract thinking, comprehension of complex ideas, and learning quickly. Others emphasize adaptation to one's environment or the ability to achieve goals in varied circumstances.

**Major theories**

- **Spearman's g factor (general intelligence):** Charles Spearman observed that people who do well on one cognitive test tend to do well on others, and proposed a common underlying factor, *g*, plus specific abilities.
- **Fluid and crystallized intelligence (Cattell-Horn):** Fluid intelligence is the ability to reason and solve novel problems; crystallized intelligence is accumulated knowledge and skills.
- **Cattell-Horn-Carroll (CHC) theory:** A hierarchical model combining many broad and narrow abilities.
- **Gardner's multiple intelligences:** Howard Gardner proposed several relatively independent intelligences (linguistic, logical-mathematical, spatial, musical, bodily-kinesthetic, interpersonal, intrapersonal, naturalistic). It is influential in education but has limited empirical support.
- **Sternberg's triarchic theory:** Intelligence consists of analytical, creative, and practical components.
- **Emotional intelligence:** The ability to perceive, understand, and manage emotions.

**Measurement**

Intelligence is commonly measured with standardized tests that yield an **intelligence quotient (IQ)**. Modern IQ tests (such as the Wechsler scales and the Stanford-Binet) are normed so that the average score is 100 with a standard deviation of 15. IQ scores are fairly stable and predict outcomes like academic achievement and job performance, but they don't capture everything, such as creativity or practical skills.

**Influences**

Intelligence is shaped by both genetics and environment. Twin and adoption studies suggest heritability is substantial (rising with age), while nutrition, education, health, and socioeconomic conditions also matter. The **Flynn effect** describes the large rise in average IQ scores across the 20th century in many countries.

**Biological basis**

Research links intelligence to brain structure and function, including brain size, the efficiency of neural networks, and especially the frontal and parietal regions (the parieto-frontal integration theory).

**Controversies**

Debates continue about the nature of intelligence, the role of genes versus environment, group differences in test scores, cultural bias in testing, and ethical uses of testing.
